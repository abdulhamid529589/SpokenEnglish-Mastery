# An Important Decision I Made Recently
### 5–7 Minutes | Simple Words, Confident Delivery

*Memorize the FLOW, not the sentences. Practice out loud 3-4 times, then just tell it like a story.*

---

## Opening — Hook First

Good morning, everyone.

Today, I would like to talk about an important decision I made recently — one that completely changed the way I think about the things I build.

A few months ago, I stumbled onto something in one of my own projects completely by accident. I was testing my website, and somehow, without even trying properly, I got past the login screen and saw data I was never supposed to see.

*(pause)*

I was not even trying to hack anything. I was just clicking around, testing features. And yet, there I was — inside a part of my own system that should have been locked tight.

*(small smile)*

That accident — finding a serious weakness in something I had proudly built with my own hands, completely by chance — is what led me to one of the most important decisions I have made recently.

---

## The Background

For the past year or so, I have been learning full-stack (FULL-stack) development — building websites, applications, complete systems from start to finish. I completed my diploma in Computer Science, and I got genuinely good at building things. Databases, backend logic, user interfaces — I could build a working system and proudly say, "yes, I made this."

*(small smile)*

And for a while, that felt like enough. I was the builder. I made things that worked. What else was there?

---

## The Decision

Then one day, I asked myself a very simple, very uncomfortable question.

*(pause)*

"I know how to build this system. But do I actually know how to break it?"

And the honest answer was — no. Not really.

That question changed everything. Because I realized something important — knowing how to build a system and knowing how to protect a system are two completely different skills. A builder makes something functional (funk-SHUN-ul). But it takes a different mindset entirely to look at your own creation and ask, "where would someone attack this? Where is it weak?"

*(pause, look at audience)*

So I made the decision — I would learn cybersecurity (CY-ber-si-KYOOR-i-tee). Not as a replacement for development, but alongside it. I wanted to become someone who does not just build systems, but also knows exactly how to break them — so that I can build them properly in the first place.

---

## Why This Decision Actually Matters

Here is the thing most people do not realize about building software. A system can look perfect on the outside — smooth design, fast performance, all the features working exactly as planned — and still have a serious weakness hiding underneath. A door that looks locked, but is not actually locked. And the only person who usually finds that weakness first is not the developer. It is an attacker.

*(pause)*

I did not want to be the developer who finds out about a weakness after something bad has already happened. I wanted to find it myself, first, before anyone else does.

Think of it like this — a locksmith (LOK-smith) does not just install locks. A great locksmith also knows exactly how a lock can be picked, because that is the only way to build one that actually holds up. That is the mindset I wanted for myself as a developer.

*(small smile)*

So now, alongside my full-stack projects, I study penetration testing (pen-i-TRAY-shun TES-ting) — which is basically the practice of trying to break into a system, legally and safely, to find its weaknesses before someone else does. I practice attacking my own projects. I try to find the cracks in my own code. It is a strange feeling the first time you successfully break something you built yourself — a mix of embarrassment and genuine excitement.

---

## Was It an Easy Decision?

Honestly? No.

Learning cybersecurity properly takes serious time — networking concepts, understanding operating systems deeply, learning how real attacks actually work. It is a completely different way of thinking compared to writing clean, working code. Some days, switching between "build mode" and "break mode" genuinely confuses my brain.

*(pause)*

But every time I find a weakness in something I built — even something small — I feel like I made the right decision. Because now, when I say I built something, I can also say, "and I made sure it is actually secure." Not just functional. Secure.

---

## Closing

So, that is the important decision I made recently — choosing to learn how to break the very things I build, so that I can build them better.

I used to think being a good developer meant knowing how to create something that works. Now I believe being a great developer means knowing how to create something that works — and cannot be easily broken.

*(pause, look at audience)*

And honestly, that one uncomfortable question I asked myself — "do I know how to break this?" — turned out to be one of the best questions I have ever asked myself.

Thank you.

---

## Delivery Tips

**Lines to say SLOWLY, with full eye contact:**
- *"There I was — inside a part of my own system that should have been locked tight."* — pause right after, let it sink in
- *"I know how to build this system. But do I actually know how to break it?"* — this is the heart of your whole presentation, let it land
- *"And the honest answer was — no. Not really."*
- *"Not just functional. Secure."*

**Lines to deliver with a small smile:**
- *"A mix of embarrassment and genuine excitement."*
- *"Some days, switching between 'build mode' and 'break mode' genuinely confuses my brain."*

**Filler words — one per section:**
- *"So"* — moving to a new section
- *"Honestly"* — before something personal
- *"Actually"* — before a surprising realization

---

## Handling Questions

- One breath. Answer in 2 sentences. Return: *"Anyway, as I was saying..."*
- Did not understand: *"Sorry, could you repeat that please?"*
- Need time: *"That is a good question, let me think..."*

---

## Likely Questions + Your Answers

**Q: What website did this actually happen on?**
A: "One of my own practice projects — a small web application I had built earlier. Nothing live or public, just something of mine I was testing."

**Q: What exactly went wrong with your project?**
A: "Honestly, I do not want to go into the exact technical detail, but basically a page that should have required login did not properly check permissions. It was a beginner-level mistake, but a real one."

**Q: What is penetration testing exactly?**
A: "It means legally trying to break into a system to find its weaknesses — before a real attacker does. Companies actually hire people to do this on purpose."

**Q: Is it not risky to learn how to break systems?**
A: "Not when it is done properly and legally, on your own systems or in authorized environments. The goal is protection, not harm."

**Q: Why not just hire a security expert later instead of learning it yourself?**
A: "Because if I understand security myself, I build safer systems from day one — instead of fixing problems after they are already built."

**Q: What was the hardest part of switching to this mindset?**
A: "Thinking like an attacker instead of a builder. When you build something, you trust it. When you attack it, you have to stop trusting it completely — that shift took time."

**Q: Do you regret spending time on full-stack development first?**
A: "Not at all. You cannot properly secure something if you do not understand how it is built. Learning to build first made learning to break things make much more sense."
