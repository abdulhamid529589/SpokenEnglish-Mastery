## 1. Opening — Hook First

Good morning, everyone.

I want to talk about a decision I made recently.

Not a dramatic decision. Not something I planned.

Actually — it started with an **accident**.

&nbsp;

`[pause — look at the audience. let the word "accident" sit for a moment.]`

&nbsp;

A few months ago, I was testing one of my own projects.

It is a **multi-vendor e-commerce website** — a platform where different sellers can list and sell their products, all in one place.

I was just clicking around. Checking pages. Testing features. Nothing special.

`[pause]`

And then — completely by accident — I ended up somewhere I was **never supposed to be**.

I bypassed a login screen. Without even trying.

I was suddenly looking at **private data**. Order details. User information. Things that were supposed to be protected.

`[pause — look at the audience. let this feel serious.]`

I just sat there, staring at the screen.

I was not hacking. I was not trying anything clever.

I just... **clicked**. And a door that should have been locked — **was not**.

`[small smile — then continue.]`

That one accidental moment is where this whole decision began.

---

## 2. Background — Who I Was Before This

To understand why that moment affected me so much — you need to know where I was at that point.

I had completed my diploma in **Computer Science**.

My entire focus was on **full-stack development** — learning how to build complete systems, from the design a user sees on the screen, all the way down to the databases and server logic running behind it.

I built projects. I fixed bugs. I celebrated every single time something finally worked after hours of frustration.

`[pause]`

I saw myself, very simply, as a **builder**. Someone who creates things. And I was proud of that.

But here is what I had never really thought about:

> **Building something and protecting something are two completely different skills.**

And before that accident — I had never been forced to confront that difference.

---

## 3. The Realization

My first reaction when I found that weakness was **embarrassment**.

I remember thinking —

> *"I built this. I spent weeks on this project. How did I miss something this basic?"*

`[pause — let the embarrassment feel real. the audience will relate.]`

But after the embarrassment passed, a much bigger — and much more uncomfortable — question took its place.

&nbsp;

> *"If I found this by accident, without even trying... what else is hiding in my code that I have not found yet?"*
>
> *"And what if someone else finds it first? Someone who does not have good intentions?"*

`[slow down here. this is the emotional turning point of the whole story.]`

&nbsp;

That question stayed with me for **days**.

Because I realized something important.

As a developer, I had been trained to ask one question:

> **"Does this feature work?"**

But I had never been trained to ask a completely different question:

> **"How could this feature be broken — on purpose?"**

Those are two entirely different mindsets.

| Mindset | The Question They Ask |
|---|---|
| **Builder** | *Does this work?* |
| **Security thinker** | *How can this be made to fail?* |

I had spent all my energy on the first question — and almost none on the second.

---

## 4. The Decision

So, I made a decision.

I decided that from that point forward, I would not just be a developer who **builds things and hopes they are safe**.

I would actively learn **cybersecurity** — not as a separate career path, but as something that fundamentally strengthens the way I build.

I wanted to understand how systems get attacked, so I could build systems that **resist** those attacks from the very beginning — instead of discovering problems by accident, the way I did with my own project.

&nbsp;

Specifically, I started learning **penetration testing**.

> Penetration testing is the practice of **legally, intentionally trying to break into systems** to discover weaknesses — before real attackers do.

&nbsp;

I started applying this directly to my own multi-vendor e-commerce project.

- I would **build** a feature — and then **deliberately try to break it**.
- Try to bypass the login again. On purpose.
- Try to access data I should not be able to reach.
- Try to manipulate forms, URLs, and requests to see if the system would let me do something it should not.

`[small smile]`

It is a strange, almost backwards feeling — spending hours building something carefully, and then spending more hours trying to **destroy your own work**.

But that is exactly the mindset I needed to develop.

---

## 5. Why This Decision Matters

Let me explain why I think this matters — beyond just my own project.

Think about how many systems you use every single day **without thinking twice**.

- Banking apps
- E-commerce websites
- Social media platforms
- Even the **college portal** you use to check your grades

Every single one of these systems was built by a developer. Just like me.

And every single one can have weaknesses — just like mine did.

`[pause]`

When you hear about a **data breach** in the news — personal information leaked, financial data stolen — most of the time, it did not happen because the attackers were unstoppable geniuses.

It happened because of a **simple mistake**. A door that looked locked — but was not.

I did not want to be someone who contributes to that problem without even realizing it.

&nbsp;

Think of it this way:

> A **locksmith** who only knows how to install locks is doing **half the job**.
>
> A truly skilled locksmith also understands exactly **how locks can be picked** — because that knowledge is what allows them to build something that actually holds up under pressure.

I wanted that same completeness in my own skills as a developer.

---

## 6. Was It Easy?

Honestly? **No. Not at all.**

Cybersecurity is not like learning a new programming framework where you can find a quick tutorial and start building within a day.

It requires:

- Understanding **networking deeply** — how data actually travels between computers
- Understanding **operating systems** at a lower level than most developers ever need to go
- Learning how real, documented **attacks have worked in the past** — and practicing in safe, legal environments

`[pause]`

There were days when switching between "building mode" and "breaking mode" **genuinely confused my brain**.

When you are building, you naturally **trust your own logic** — you assume things will work the way you designed them.

But when you switch to attacking your own system, you have to **completely let go of that trust**. You have to assume everything is broken, until you personally prove otherwise.

That mental shift — more than any technical skill — was the hardest part of this whole decision.

`[small smile]`

But every single time I find a weakness in something I built — even something small, even something embarrassing —

I feel a strange mix of **frustration** and **genuine satisfaction**.

- Frustration — because I made a mistake.
- Satisfaction — because I found it **myself**, before it could ever hurt anyone.

---

## 7. How It Changed My Daily Habits

This decision did not just add a new skill to my resume.

It **changed how I spend my time every single day**.

Alongside my regular full-stack development work, I now dedicate real, consistent time specifically to cybersecurity practice — not just watching tutorials, but **actually practicing**. Setting up safe environments. Legally attempting to break things. Learning from the process.

It has become part of my **identity** as a developer — not a separate hobby on the side.

&nbsp;

When I build a new feature now, my very first instinct — **before I even celebrate that it works** — is to ask myself:

&nbsp;

> ## *"Okay. Now — how would I break this?"*

&nbsp;

`[say this line slowly. like you are genuinely thinking it. look up from your notes.]`

That single habit — asking **"how would I break this?"** right after building something — is probably the single biggest change this decision brought into my daily life.

---

## 8. Closing

So, to summarize —

The important decision I made recently was choosing to **learn how to break the very systems I build**, so that I could learn to build them properly in the first place.

It started with an accident. A login screen that should have stopped me — but did not.

And that small, embarrassing moment taught me something I believe every developer eventually needs to learn:

&nbsp;

> **Building something functional and building something secure are two completely different achievements. And only one of them is optional.**

&nbsp;

`[pause. look at the audience. let this land.]`

I used to believe that being a good developer meant knowing how to create something that **works**.

Now I believe that being a **great** developer means knowing how to create something that works — and **cannot be easily broken** by someone who wishes to cause harm.

And honestly, that one accidental click — the one that let me somewhere I was never supposed to go —

turned out to be one of the most **valuable accidents** of my entire learning journey.

&nbsp;

**Thank you.**
