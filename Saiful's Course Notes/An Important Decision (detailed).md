# An Important Decision I Made Recently
### Comprehensive Version | 8–10 Minutes | Deep, Detailed, Still Simple Words

*This is the longer, richer version — more story, more detail, more reflection. Read it 4-5 times out loud before class. Memorize the FLOW, not the exact sentences.*

---

## Opening — Hook First

Good morning, everyone.

Today, I would like to talk about an important decision I made recently — a decision that did not happen in one dramatic moment, but one that completely changed the direction of how I see myself as a developer.

Let me take you back to where it started.

A few months ago, I was testing one of my own projects — a multi-vendor (MUL-ti VEN-dor) e-commerce (ee-KOM-mers) website I had been building, the kind of website where many different sellers can list and sell their own products, all on one platform. I was just clicking around, checking different pages, testing features, the way any developer does before showing their work to someone else.

*(pause)*

And then, completely by accident, I ended up somewhere I was never supposed to be. I bypassed a login screen without even trying to. I was suddenly looking at information that should have been protected — order details, user data, things that were meant to be private.

*(pause, look at audience)*

For a second, I just sat there, staring at the screen, not fully understanding what had just happened. I was not hacking. I was not trying anything clever. I just... clicked, and a door that should have been locked simply was not.

*(small smile)*

That one accidental moment is where this whole decision began.

---

## The Background — Who I Was Before This

To understand why this moment affected me so much, you need to understand where I was in my journey at that point.

I had completed my diploma in Computer Science, and for a long time, my entire focus had been on full-stack (FULL-stack) development — learning how to build complete systems, from the visual design a user sees, all the way down to the databases and server logic running behind the scenes. I built projects. I debugged errors. I celebrated every time something finally worked after hours of frustration.

I saw myself, very simply, as a builder. Someone who creates things. And I was proud of that identity. When something worked — when a feature functioned exactly as planned — that feeling of accomplishment (uh-KOM-plish-ment) was, honestly, addictive.

*(pause)*

But building something and protecting something are two very different skills. And before that accidental moment, I had never really been forced to confront that difference.

---

## The Realization

After I found that weakness, my first reaction was actually embarrassment. I remember thinking — I built this. I spent weeks on this project. How did I miss something this basic?

*(pause)*

But after the embarrassment passed, a much bigger, more uncomfortable question took its place.

"If I found this by accident, without even trying — what else is hiding in my code that I have not found yet? And more importantly, what if someone else finds it first? Someone who does not have good intentions?"

That question sat with me for days. Because I realized something important — as a developer, I had been trained to think about one thing: does this feature work correctly? But I had never been trained to think about a completely different question: how could this feature be misused, broken, or exploited (ik-SPLOY-ted)?

Those are two entirely different mindsets. A builder asks, "does this work?" A security-minded person asks, "how could this be made to fail on purpose?" And I realized I had spent all my energy on the first question, and almost none on the second.

---

## The Decision

So, I made a decision.

I decided that from that point forward, I would not just be a developer who builds things and hopes they are safe. I would actively learn cybersecurity (CY-ber-si-KYOOR-i-tee) — not as a separate career path, but as something that fundamentally strengthens the way I build.

I wanted to understand how systems get attacked, so that I could build systems that resist those attacks from the very beginning, instead of discovering problems by accident, the way I did with my own project.

*(pause)*

Specifically, I started learning penetration testing (pen-i-TRAY-shun TES-ting) — the practice of legally, intentionally trying to break into systems to discover weaknesses before real attackers do. I began studying how attackers think, what common mistakes developers make, and how those small mistakes can turn into serious problems.

I started applying this knowledge directly to my own multi-vendor e-commerce project. I would build a feature, and then — deliberately, this time — try to break it myself. Try to bypass the login again, on purpose. Try to access data I should not be able to access. Try to manipulate forms, URLs, and requests to see if the system would let me do something it should not.

*(small smile)*

It is a strange, almost backwards feeling — spending hours building something carefully, and then spending more hours trying to destroy your own work. But that is exactly the mindset I needed to develop.

---

## Why This Decision Actually Matters

Let me explain why I believe this decision matters, beyond just my own project.

Think about how many systems we use every single day without thinking twice — banking apps, e-commerce websites, social media platforms, even the small college portal we use to check grades. Every single one of these systems was built by developers just like me. And every single one of these systems can have weaknesses, exactly like the one I found in mine.

*(pause)*

Most of the time, when we hear about a data breach (DAY-tuh BREECH) in the news — someone's personal information leaked, financial data stolen — it did not happen because attackers were unstoppable geniuses. Very often, it happened because of a simple mistake, similar to the one I found in my own project. A door that looked locked but was not.

I did not want to be someone who contributes to that problem without even realizing it. I wanted to be someone who catches these mistakes before they cause real harm to real people.

Think of it like this — a locksmith (LOK-smith) who only knows how to install locks is doing half the job. A truly skilled locksmith also understands exactly how locks can be picked, because that knowledge is what allows them to build something that actually holds up under pressure. I wanted that same completeness in my own skills as a developer.

---

## Was It an Easy Decision?

Honestly? No. Not at all.

Learning cybersecurity properly is not like learning a new programming framework where you can find a quick tutorial and start building within a day. It requires understanding networking deeply — how data actually travels between computers. It requires understanding operating systems at a much lower level than most developers ever need to go. It requires learning how real, documented attacks have worked in the past, and practicing techniques in safe, legal, controlled environments.

*(pause)*

There were days when switching between "building mode" and "breaking mode" genuinely confused my brain. When you are building something, you naturally trust your own logic — you assume things will work the way you designed them. But when you switch to attacking your own system, you have to completely abandon that trust. You have to assume everything is broken until you personally prove otherwise.

That mental shift, more than any technical skill, was the hardest part of this decision.

*(small smile)*

But every single time I find a weakness in something I built — even something small, even something embarrassing — I feel a strange mix of frustration and genuine satisfaction. Frustration, because I made a mistake. Satisfaction, because I found it myself, before it could ever hurt anyone.

---

## How This Decision Changed My Daily Habits

This decision did not just add a new skill to my resume — it actually changed how I spend my time every single day.

Alongside my regular full-stack development work on my e-commerce project and my ongoing learning of system design (SIS-tem di-ZINE), I now dedicate real, consistent time specifically to cybersecurity practice. I watch tutorials, but more importantly, I practice hands-on — setting up small, safe environments where I can legally attempt to break systems and learn from the process.

It has become part of my identity as a developer, not a separate hobby on the side. When I build a new feature now, my very first instinct, before I even celebrate that it works, is to ask myself: "okay, now how would I break this?"

*(pause)*

That single habit — asking "how would I break this?" immediately after building something — is probably the single biggest change this decision brought into my daily life.

---

## Closing

So, to summarize — the important decision I made recently was choosing to learn how to break the very systems I build, so that I could learn to build them properly in the first place.

It started with an accident — a login screen that should have stopped me, but did not. And that small, embarrassing moment taught me something I believe every developer eventually needs to learn: that building something functional (funk-SHUN-ul) and building something secure are two completely different achievements, and only one of them is optional.

*(pause, look at audience)*

I used to believe that being a good developer meant knowing how to create something that works. Now, I believe that being a great developer means knowing how to create something that works, and cannot be easily broken by someone who wishes to cause harm.

And honestly, that one accidental click — the one that let me somewhere I was never supposed to go — turned out to be one of the most valuable accidents of my entire learning journey.

Thank you.

## Likely Questions + Detailed Answers

**Q: What exactly was the weakness in your project?**
A: "Without going into too much technical detail, a page that should have required proper login checks did not verify permissions correctly. It is actually a very common mistake, especially for developers early in their journey."

**Q: Was any real, live data exposed to other people?**
A: "No — this was on my own personal practice project, not a live, public website with real users. That is actually part of why I feel comfortable sharing this story openly."

**Q: What is penetration testing exactly?**
A: "It is the practice of legally and intentionally trying to break into a system to find its weaknesses, before a real attacker with bad intentions ever gets the chance."

**Q: Is it not risky to learn how to break systems?**
A: "Not when it is done properly, legally, and on systems you own or are authorized to test. The entire goal is protection, not causing harm."

**Q: How is this different from just being a careful developer?**
A: "Being careful helps you avoid mistakes you already know about. Cybersecurity training teaches you to actively search for mistakes you do not even know exist yet."

**Q: Why not just hire a security expert later instead of learning it yourself?**
A: "Because if I understand security myself, I build safer systems from the very first line of code — instead of discovering expensive problems after the system is already built and in use."

**Q: What was the hardest part of switching to this mindset?**
A: "Learning to stop trusting my own work. As a builder, you naturally trust your own logic. As someone testing for security, you have to assume everything is broken until you personally prove otherwise."

**Q: Do you regret spending time on full-stack development first, instead of starting with cybersecurity?**
A: "Not at all. You cannot properly secure something if you do not deeply understand how it is built. Learning to build first is exactly what made learning to break things make real sense to me."

**Q: Has this decision changed how you build new projects now?**
A: "Yes, completely. Now, right after I finish building any new feature, my first instinct is to ask myself how I could break it — before I even celebrate that it works."
