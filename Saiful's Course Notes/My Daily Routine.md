# My Daily Routine — Presentation Script (v3: Full, Pro, Simple, Funny)

*Tip: Don't memorize word-for-word. Learn the FLOW. Read this 4-5 times out loud, then just tell it like a story.*

---

## Opening

Good morning, everyone. Today I'm going to talk about my daily routine — and I promise, it's not as boring as it sounds. Well, maybe a little.

Let me start with a quick introduction. I recently completed (com-PLEET-id) my diploma in Computer Science, and right now, I'm preparing (pri-PAIR-ing) for a developer job. So most of my day is built around learning, coding, and slowly turning into the developer I want to become. Some days it feels like progress. Some days it feels like my laptop is winning.

## Morning — Slow Start, Fast Brain

My day usually begins with waking up and freshening (FRESH-en-ing) up — though, like most people, I do enjoy a few extra minutes in bed before actually getting up. After that, I have breakfast, which is nothing fancy, just simple fuel for the brain. After that, I walk around in our garden for a few minutes. I like to call it my "morning meeting with nature" — no agenda, no laptop, just fresh air.

Then I sit down with my laptop, and the real work begins. My main project right now is a multi-vendor (MUL-ti VEN-dor) e-commerce (ee-KOM-mers) website. In simple words — it's a website where many sellers can sell their own products, all on one platform. Think of it like a digital marketplace, with many shops under one big roof. It sounds simple, but building it is not simple at all.

Alongside (uh-LONG-side) that project, I'm learning system design (SIS-tem di-ZINE). Honestly, this topic is tough. Very tough. If coding is like cooking one dish well, system design is like planning a whole restaurant — kitchen, staff, orders, delivery, everything, all working together, all at once. I'm still learning how to run that restaurant without it catching fire.

## Mid-Morning — The Human Reset Button

After working for a while, my brain needs a small reset. So I take a short break — maybe a YouTube podcast (POD-cast), or a movie. It's not laziness, it's "strategic recharging." At least that's what I tell myself.

## Afternoon — Switching Hats

In the afternoon, I have a light snack and rest a little. Then I switch my focus to cybersecurity (CY-ber-si-KYOOR-i-tee). I watch tutorials (TOO-tor-ee-uhls), and I practice hands-on along with them. It's a completely different mindset from building websites — instead of building something, I'm learning how to protect it from people who want to break it. A bit like being a builder in the morning and a security guard in the afternoon.

After lunch, I usually take a short nap. Not because I'm lazy — because even developers need to "restart the system" sometimes.

## Evening — Slowing Down, On Purpose

Later in the day, I go out for a walk to refresh (ri-FRESH) my mind. In the evening, after a snack, I sit down with a few non-academic (non-a-ka-DEM-ik) books. Right now, that includes my spoken English materials, since I'm attending this very course. I also read other books when I get the chance — my brain likes variety, apparently.

After that, I go back to my laptop and continue working for a while. Yes, the laptop and I have a long relationship. It's complicated, but we make it work.

## Weekly Rhythm — Not Every Day Looks the Same

My week isn't just one repeated day on loop, though. Every now and then, I go out with friends to different places, just to keep my mind fresh — because staring at code for too long can turn anyone a little strange.

And on Fridays, I take a proper rest day. I scroll through Facebook, watch funny videos, documentaries (dok-yoo-MEN-ta-rees), and interesting blogs on YouTube. It's basically my brain's weekend vacation, even if my body never leaves the house.

## Closing — Honest and Confident

At night, after dinner, I spend a little time on my phone, and then I go to sleep — usually still thinking about that one bug in my code that refused to get fixed.

If I'm honest, my day doesn't follow a strict clock, and that's something I'm still improving. But it does follow a rhythm — every day, I try to touch three things: my e-commerce project, system design, and cybersecurity practice. It's still a work in progress. But then again, so is every good developer.

Thank you.

## How to sound natural, not memorized

- **Pause slightly** between sections — like you're thinking, not reciting.
- Let the funny lines land — say them a little slower, with a small smile. Comedy needs timing, even in English class.
- Use small natural fillers occasionally: *"So,"* *"Actually,"* *"Honestly,"* *"You know"* — but don't overuse them.

**Where to naturally drop them into this exact script:**
- *"So most of my day is built around learning, coding, and slowly turning into the developer I want to become."* (start of a new idea → "So")
- *"Honestly, this topic is tough. Very tough."* (before admitting something → "Honestly" fits best here)
- *"You know, staring at code for too long can turn anyone a little strange."* (before a light joke → "You know" softens it, makes it feel spoken, not written)
- *"Honestly, it's a challenge."* (in the Q&A answer about time management — fits perfectly as-is)

**Rule of thumb:** one filler per section, max. If every sentence has "so" or "actually," it starts to sound like a nervous habit instead of natural speech. Silence is also fine — you don't need a filler in every pause.
- Slow down on the real, sincere lines ("still a work in progress," "I find it tough") — that contrast between funny and honest is what makes a presentation memorable.
- If you forget an exact line, just say the idea in your own simple words. Nobody in the room has this script memorized except you.

## Handling questions in the middle

- **Pause, don't panic** — one second of silence is completely normal.
- **Answer in 1–3 sentences**, then return to your topic: *"...and after that, as I was saying, I go back to my project."*
- If you don't understand: *"Sorry, could you repeat that, please?"* — perfectly good English, not a failure.
- If you're stuck: *"That's a good question, let me think..."* — buys you a second and sounds natural.

## Likely questions + simple answers

**Q: Why don't you have a fixed time for your routine?**
A: "Actually, I'm still working on that. Right now I follow whatever feels productive, but I know a fixed schedule would help me focus better."

**Q: What is a multi-vendor e-commerce website?**
A: "It's an online shopping website where many sellers can list and sell their own products — like Daraz or Amazon, but many shops in one place."

**Q: Why do you find system design tough?**
A: "Because it's not just writing code — it's planning how a big application works for many users at once. It needs a different way of thinking."

**Q: What is backend development?**
A: "It's the part of a website users don't see — the server and database that make everything work behind the scenes."

**Q: Why are you learning cybersecurity if you're a developer?**
A: "Because a good developer should also understand security — how to protect an app from attacks, and write safer code from the start."

**Q: How do you manage your time between so many things?**
A: "Honestly, it's a challenge. I don't follow strict hours, I just try to give time to each area every day, even a small amount."

**Q: What is your future goal?**
A: "My goal is to become a job-ready developer, and later understand not just coding, but how big systems work together."

**Q: Do you rest on weekdays too, or only Friday?**
A: "Mostly just Friday. On other days, I take small breaks, like a podcast or short video, to keep my mind fresh."

**Q: Why are you learning spoken English now?**
A: "Because good communication is as important as technical skill, especially for interviews and teamwork. I want to be confident when I speak."

**Q: If your teacher asks something totally random (like your favorite food):**
A: Just answer honestly and simply. These questions test if you can respond naturally in English — not your technical knowledge.
