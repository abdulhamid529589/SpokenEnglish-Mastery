## 2. What Is a Modal Verb? (Core Concept)

**Definition:** A Modal Verb is a **type of verb** (like helping verbs) that is used as a **shortcut/substitute** for a longer phrase, to directly show the **mood, condition, or state** of the subject — such as ability, possibility, advice, obligation, permission, or a polite request — without having to write a long sentence.

### How the concept was illustrated:

1. **Example 1 (Passive Voice → Modal):**
   - Long sentence: *"He is capable to do this work."*
   - Shortened using a modal: *"He CAN do this work."*
   - **CAN** replaces "is capable to" — shows **ability**.

2. **Example 2 (Passive Voice → Modal):**
   - Long sentence: *"You are advised to work hard."* (Passive Voice)
   - Shortened using a modal: *"You SHOULD work hard."*
   - **SHOULD** replaces "are advised to" — shows **advice**.

**Conclusion:** Modal verbs are words that show:
- **Possibility** (chance of something happening)
- **Ability** (capability/capacity)
- **Advice** (recommendation)
- **Obligation** (duty)
- **Offer**
- **Polite request**
- **Necessity**

> These are words that reveal the **mood/condition/state** of the subject regarding an action — hence called **Modal Verbs**.

**List of Modal Verbs covered in this session:**
can, may, could, might, should, must, ought to, has to/have to/had to, need (to), would, will, shall, used to.

## 3. Structure Rule for Modal Verbs

**Key structural rule:** A modal verb and a helping verb (is/am/are/was/were/has/have/had) **cannot be used together** in the same verb slot. You use **either** a modal verb **or** a helping verb — never both simultaneously.

- ❌ *He is can work.* / ❌ *He can was...*
- ✅ *He can work.*

### Sentence structure with modals:

**Subject + Modal Verb + Main Verb (base form) + Object**

Examples:
- *I can work hard.* → Subject (I) + Modal (can) + Main Verb (work) + rest of sentence.
- *She should sleep now.* → Subject (She) + Modal (should) + Main Verb (sleep) + rest.
- *He may be there.* → Subject (He) + Modal (may) + Main Verb (be) + rest.

**Important:** No helping verb (is/am/are/was/were/has/have) appears alongside the modal verb in this structure.

## 4. Modal Verb: CAN

**Core meaning:** CAN shows **ability/capability** (strength/power to do something) and also **permission** (informal).

### Uses of CAN:

1. **Ability/Capability** (primary and most important use)
   - *I can speak English.*
   - *It can help you to achieve good marks.*
   - *India can win the World Cup.*
   - *Can you read this?*
   - *Can CSK win IPL?*
   - *Can you get admission in DU?*
   - *I can do whatever I want in my life.*

2. **Permission (informal)** — used with friends, family, or casually with strangers
   - *Can I come?*
   - *Can I go?*
   - *Can I ask you a question?*
   - *Can I say something?*
   - *You can come in.*
   - *Can I do something for you?*
   - *You cannot go outside.* (negative)
   - *You cannot ask me such questions.* (negative)

**Important exam rule:**
> There is **only ONE modal verb** in the entire English language that shows **ability/capability/capacity** — and that is **CAN**. No other modal can substitute for this meaning.

**Trick to remember:** CAN = ability/power (primary) + informal permission (secondary).

---

## 5. Modal Verb: MAY

**Core meaning:** MAY shows **formal permission**, **possibility**, and is also used to give **blessings/wishes**.

### Uses of MAY:

1. **Formal Permission** (used out of respect, with strangers, seniors, teachers, in professional/formal settings — NOT with close friends/family)
   - *May I come in, Sir?*
   - *May I go to the washroom?*
   - *May I ask you a question?*
   - *May I help you?* (commonly seen at hotel receptions — "How may I help you?")
   - *May I know your name?* (used with strangers)
   - *May I talk to you?*

   **Formal vs. Informal — key distinction:**
   | Formal (out of respect, unfamiliar/professional relationship) | Informal (with friends, family, known people) |
   |---|---|
   | MAY | CAN |
   | *May I ask your name?* (to a stranger) | *Can I ask you a question?* (to a friend) |
   | *May I help you?* (customer service) | *Can I help you?* (casual, less standard in formal customer service contexts) |

   Note: In real/practical usage, people sometimes use "Can I help you?" casually, but grammatically and formally, **"May I help you?"** is preferred with strangers/customers due to the implied respect and lack of familiarity.

2. **Possibility**
   - *It may rain today.*
   - *Rajeev may be upset with you.*
   - *He may be there.*

3. **Blessings / Wishes**
   - *May God bless you.*
   - *May you live long.*
   - *May you have a happy birthday.*
   - *May your dreams come true.*

**Trick to remember:** MAY = formal permission + possibility + blessings/wishes.

## 6. Modal Verb: COULD

**Core meaning:** COULD is the **past tense of CAN** — showing past ability. It is also used for **possibility** and **polite requests**.

### Uses of COULD:

1. **Past Ability** (main use — primary meaning)
   - *I can speak English* (present ability) → *I could speak English* (past ability — "I was able to / I could speak English then").
   - *I could meet you* (I was able to meet you).
   - *I could not meet him.* (I was not able to meet him.)
   - *I could not understand.* (I was not able to understand / I couldn't understand.)
   - *I could understand.* (I was able to understand.)
   - *She could come.*
   - *I am happy that you could come.*
   - *They could not reach on time.* (past — they were unable to reach on time.)

   **Important nuance clarified by the instructor:** Whether translated as "sака" (was able to) or "पाया" (managed to) in Hindi, both convey the same essence — **the ability to do something in the past**. There's no need to rigidly stick to one specific Hindi translation; the core meaning (ability, past tense) is what matters.
   - Example: *I could speak Chinese* — this can mean either "I was able to speak Chinese" or "I managed to speak Chinese" — both interpretations are grammatically valid because the essence/crux of "could" is about **past ability**.

2. **Possibility**
   - *They could be at home.*
   - *He could come by bus.*

3. **Polite Request** (very common, more formal/polite than "can")
   - *Could I use your phone?*
   - *Could you please tell me about this?*
   - *Could you pass the bottle, please?* (famous example — from the movie *Taare Zameen Par*)
   - *Could I get some water, please?*

**Trick to remember:** COULD = past tense of CAN (ability) + possibility + polite request (often paired with "please").

---

## 7. Modal Verb: MIGHT

**Core meaning:** MIGHT is the **past tense of MAY**, used mainly for **possibility (informal, weaker/lower probability)** and giving **suggestions**.

### Uses of MIGHT:

1. **Possibility (informal, lower probability)**
   - *Your bag might be in the room.*
   - *I might go with you.*

2. **Suggestions** (main exam-relevant use)
   - *You might visit the garden.*
   - *You might try this sweet.*

3. **Formal/polite request (rare, British usage)**
   - *Might I borrow your pen?* (uncommon, mainly used by the British)

**Trick to remember:** MIGHT = past tense of MAY; mainly used for **giving suggestions** and **low-probability possibility** (informal).

---

## 8. Possibility Strength Scale (Could vs. May vs. Might)

The instructor gave a rough percentage-based scale to distinguish possibility strength among modals:

| Modal | Possibility Strength |
|---|---|
| **COULD** | ~90% (very high possibility) |
| **MAY** | ~50–60% (moderate/balanced possibility — "50-50" is the essence of possibility) |
| **MIGHT** | Below 30% (informal, rare, low possibility) |

**Note:** MAY is used most frequently for possibility since possibility inherently means "50-50" chance. MIGHT is comparatively rare in exams.

---

## 9. Modal Verb: SHOULD

**Core meaning:** SHOULD shows **advice/suggestion** ("चाहिए" — "should/ought to") and also mild **probability/expectation**.

### Uses of SHOULD:

1. **Advice/Suggestion**
   - *You should join a gym.*
   - *You should not meet him.*
   - *What should I wear?*

2. **Probability/Expectation** (mild, subjective opinion — "I think this should be the case")
   - *He should be here soon.*
   - *It should not cost more than that.*
   - *It should be snowing now.*

**Important exam note:** SHOULD is considered the **least priority/least strong** modal for situations requiring stronger obligation. It's used for **general advice**, not urgent or mandatory necessity (see comparison with MUST below).

**Trick to remember:** SHOULD = general advice/suggestion, softer than MUST.

---

## 10. Modal Verb: MUST

**Core meaning:** MUST shows **strong obligation, moral duty, or logical certainty** — something that absolutely has to happen, with **no other option**.

> **Exam importance:** The instructor emphasized that MUST is the **most important modal verb** from an exam perspective — very frequently tested in CUET and other competitive exams.

### Uses of MUST:

1. **Obligation / Moral Duty / Recommendation** (mandatory, no choice left)
   - *You must go to sleep now.*
   - *You must keep the traffic rules in your mind.*
   - Example given: If someone is **vomiting blood** (a serious medical emergency), the correct modal is **MUST** (not "should") — *He must consult a doctor* — because it's urgent and non-negotiable, not just optional advice.

2. **Speculation / Logical Certainty** (when you are very sure/certain about something — "must be" = "it has to be the case")
   - *He must be joking.* (= He is probably joking — a logical/certain guess.)
   - *There must be some mistake.* (= There has to be some mistake — logically deduced.)
   - *Priya must be in some trouble.*
   - *It must be him* (e.g., inferring who's knocking at the door).
   - *It must be Aarti* (guessing who's behind a closed door based on context).
   - *It must be very hot.* (When you're certain/sure about a statement.)

**Trick to remember:** MUST = strong necessity/obligation (no other option) OR logical certainty (you're very sure something is true).

---

## 11. Modal Verb: OUGHT TO

**Core meaning:** OUGHT TO is used for **ideal things** — what "should" ideally happen, in a more **formal** register. It can be easily replaced by SHOULD in most contexts.

### Uses of OUGHT TO:

- *We ought to drink fresh juice.* (Ideally, we should — but practically, people drink packaged juice instead.)
- *You ought to come on time.* (Ideally you should be punctual and diligent, but you're not.)
- *They ought to take precautions.*
- *You ought to take care of your parents.*
- *You ought to respect elders.*
- *You ought to obey the traffic rules.*
- *You ought to have nutritious food.*

**Trick to remember:** OUGHT TO = formal version of SHOULD, specifically used for **idealistic things** (what "ought" to happen in an ideal world, even if it doesn't practically happen).

---

## 12. SHOULD vs. MUST vs. OUGHT TO — Comparison

| Modal | Strength/Priority | Best Used For |
|---|---|---|
| **MUST** | Highest — mandatory, no option left | Urgent obligation, strong necessity, logical certainty |
| **OUGHT TO** | Second — formal, idealistic | Formal/idealistic recommendations (found more in books/formal writing) |
| **SHOULD** | Least/weakest priority | General, casual advice/suggestion |

### Worked example distinguishing SHOULD vs. MUST:

- *He is ill* (mildly sick) → *He should consult a doctor.* (general advice)
- *He is very ill* (seriously sick, e.g., vomiting blood) → *He must consult a doctor.* (urgent, mandatory — no option, logically certain need)
- *She should work hard* (general advice for someone about studies).
- *She got failed in the exam last year* → *She must work hard.* (Now it's not optional advice — it's a strong necessity/obligation given the situation.)

**Rule for guidelines/manuals/laws:** Whenever a sentence involves a **rule, law, guideline, or manual** (e.g., hospital notices, safety instructions), **MUST** is the mandatory/appropriate choice — not SHOULD.
- Example: *"One must not smoke here."* (hospital notice)
- Example: *"You must fasten your seatbelt."* (mandatory safety rule)

## 13. Modal Verb: HAS TO / HAVE TO / HAD TO

**Core meaning:** Shows **compulsion, obligation, or lack of choice/option** — very similar to MUST in meaning.

### Uses of HAS TO / HAVE TO / HAD TO:

- *She has to go.* (She has no option; she must go.)
- *You have to understand that.*
- *Do you have to go there regularly?*
- *We had to stop him.* (past)
- *She does not have to do this.* (negative — no obligation)

**Trick to remember:** HAS TO/HAVE TO/HAD TO = compulsion/no other option (functions almost identically to MUST, but conjugates with tense: has/have for present, had for past, will have to/shall have to for future).

---

## 14. Modal Verb: NEED (TO)

**Core meaning:** NEED shows **requirement/necessity** — often interchangeable with HAS TO/HAVE TO/HAD TO.

### Uses of NEED (TO):

- *I need to go.* (I have a requirement to go.)
- *He needs to make a phone call.*
- *You need not worry at all.* (negative — no need/requirement)
- *You need to come.*
- *You need to understand my point.*

**Trick to remember:** NEED (TO) = shows requirement/necessity; closely related to (and often replaceable with) has to/have to/had to.

---

## 15. Modal Verb: WOULD

**Core meaning:** WOULD has **four main uses** — polite request, offer, past habit, and hypothetical/unreal situations.

> **Exam importance:** Highlighted as one of the **most important modal verbs** for the exam.

### Uses of WOULD:

1. **Polite Request** (similar to "could," but "could" is often paired more with "please")
   - *I would like to say something.*
   - *I would like to ask a question.*
   - *I would like to bring some coffee for you.*
   - *I would like to understand this.*
   - (Spoken form contraction: "I'd like to..." instead of full "I would like to...")

2. **Offer**
   - *Would you like some chocolates?*
   - *Would you like some coffee?*
   - *Would you give me some time?*
   - *Would you have something?*
   - *I would be happy to help you.*

3. **Past Habit**
   - *In my childhood, I would drink milk.* (= I used to drink milk when I was young.)
   - *When I was little, I would play cricket.* (= I used to play cricket when I was little.)

4. **Hypothetical/Unreal Situation** (imaginary, wishful, or uncertain scenarios — replaces WILL when talking about something uncertain, imagined, or not actually happening)
   - *We would go to Canada, but we are too busy.* (An imagined scenario — not actually happening.)
   - *I am in trouble, but you would not understand.* (= You wouldn't understand — a hypothetical guess, not something actually tested/tried.)
   - *If I won the bet, I would travel the world.* (Hypothetical — the bet hasn't been won.)
   - *If you ask me, I would say...* (Hypothetical — conditional on something not yet happened.)
   - *I would not do this.* (compare to *I will not do this* — see WILL vs. WOULD below.)

**Trick to remember:** WOULD = polite request + offer + past habit + hypothetical/unreal/imagined situations.

---

## 16. WILL vs. WOULD

**Key distinction:**

| WILL | WOULD |
|---|---|
| Shows **certainty/surety** — something you are sure/confirmed about | Shows **hypothetical/uncertain/imagined** situations — not confirmed, "hawa mein baat" (something speculative/in the air) |
| *I will meet you at the railway station.* (= I am sure I will meet you.) | *I would meet you at the railway station.* (= hypothetical, uncertain — "I would meet you..." implying it's not confirmed/actual) |
| *I will not do this.* (= I'm sure/certain I won't do this.) | *I would not do this.* (= speculative — an assumption or imagined scenario, not necessarily grounded in trying/testing it) |

**Trick to remember:** WILL = certainty (you're sure this will happen). WOULD = hypothetical/imagined/uncertain (you're speculating, not confirming).

---

## 17. WILL vs. SHALL

**Standard rule:**
- **SHALL** is used with **I** and **WE** (for simple future / future indefinite tense).
  - *I shall come there.*
  - *She will go to market.* (with other subjects, WILL is used)

**Exception — Rule Reversal:**
When a sentence expresses:
- **Determination** (a firm resolve/intention),
- **Threat**, or
- **Promise**,

then the rule **reverses**: **WILL** is used with **I** and **WE**, and **SHALL** is used with the rest.

- *Will you bring this change? Yes, I will.* (determination)
- *I will kill you.* (threat — WILL used with "I" due to strong determination/threat context)

**Trick to remember:** Normal future = SHALL with I/We, WILL with others. Determination/Threat/Promise = rule reverses → WILL with I/We.

---

## 18. USED TO (Past Habit)

**Core meaning:** USED TO directly shows a **past habit** — something you regularly did in the past but no longer do.

### Uses of USED TO:

- *I used to walk daily.* (Clearly shows past habit — I regularly walked in the past.)
- *I used to go for a walk.*

**Trick to remember:** USED TO = direct, standalone indicator of past habit — no additional context/hint sentence needed before it.

---

## 19. WOULD vs. USED TO — Key Difference

Both WOULD and USED TO can show past habits, and are often interchangeable — **but** there's a subtle difference:

| WOULD (for past habit) | USED TO (for past habit) |
|---|---|
| Requires a **prior hint/context** establishing that the sentence is about the past (e.g., "When I was in my town...", "In my childhood...") | Can be used **directly**, without needing a preceding hint — it inherently signals past habit on its own |
| *When I was in my town, I would go for a walk.* | *I used to go for a walk.* (direct, no extra context needed) |

**Trick to remember:** WOULD needs a contextual "hint" (time marker) before it to indicate past habit; USED TO stands alone and directly conveys past habit.

## 20. Master Summary Table of All Modal Verbs

| Modal Verb | Primary Use(s) |
|---|---|
| **CAN** | Ability/capability (the ONLY modal for this); informal permission |
| **MAY** | Formal permission; possibility; blessings/wishes |
| **COULD** | Past ability (past tense of CAN); possibility; polite request |
| **MIGHT** | Past tense of MAY; suggestions; low/informal possibility |
| **SHOULD** | General advice/suggestion; mild probability/expectation (least priority) |
| **MUST** | Strong obligation/moral duty (no option); logical certainty/speculation (most exam-important) |
| **OUGHT TO** | Formal version of SHOULD; idealistic recommendations |
| **HAS TO / HAVE TO / HAD TO** | Compulsion/obligation, no other option (present/past) |
| **NEED (TO)** | Requirement/necessity |
| **WOULD** | Polite request; offer; past habit (with hint); hypothetical/unreal situations |
| **WILL** | Certainty/surety about the future; simple future (with subjects other than I/We, normally) |
| **SHALL** | Simple future with I/We (normal rule); reverses to WILL+I/We under determination/threat/promise |
| **USED TO** | Direct past habit (no hint needed) |

## 21. Practice Questions with Explanations

1. **___ you pass with flying colours.** (Options: may, should, must, could)
   → **MAY** — this is a **blessing/wish** ("May you pass with flying colours"), not advice (should), not obligation (must), and not a polite request (could — since nothing is being asked for/requested here).

2. **It is cloudy. It ___ rain soon.** (Options: may, can, might, could)
   → **MAY** — shows possibility ("it might/may rain" — a chance, not a certainty).

3. **His condition is not good. He ___ see a doctor immediately.** (Options: should, must)
   → **MUST** — the word "immediately" is a hint indicating urgency/no other option — a critical situation requiring MUST, not just general advice (should).

4. **This is the house where I ___ live in my childhood.** (Options: used to, dare to, need to)
   → **USED TO** — "in my childhood" is a clear hint indicating past habit; "used to" directly shows this. ("Dare to" is for courage; "need to" implies requirement — neither fits.)

5. **We ___ help the poor and needy.** (Options: will, shall, ought to)
   → **OUGHT TO** — this expresses an **idealistic** duty/expectation (helping the poor is the "right" or "ideal" thing to do), which is the specific domain of "ought to," even though "will/shall help" is grammatically fine on its own, it doesn't capture the idealistic essence intended here.

6. **I wish I ___ help you, but I have no money.** (Options: may, could)
   → **COULD** — used with "I wish" to express a wish about ability ("I wish I could help you" = I wish I had the ability to help you).

7. **Measles ___ be quite dangerous.** (Options: can, could, should, would)
   → **CAN** — this is a **proven fact** (not a possibility/guess), so CAN (showing inherent capability/nature of the disease) is correct, not COULD (which would suggest mere possibility rather than an established fact).

8. **___ you please show me the way to the station?** (Options: could, will, should, can)
   → **COULD** (also acceptable: **CAN** in practical usage) — this is a **polite request** (indicated by "please"). Both "could" and "can" can be considered correct in real-world usage, but grammatically "could" is the more standard/preferred choice for polite requests.

9. **One ___ abide by the laws.** (Options: may, could, should, must)
   → **MUST** (though "should" is also grammatically plausible) — since this reflects a **law/guideline/mandatory rule**, MUST is more appropriate than SHOULD in formal contexts involving laws, manuals, or guidelines (e.g., *"One must not smoke here"*, *"You must fasten your seatbelt"*).

10. **___ I have another cup of tea? It is very chilly outside.** (Options: will, can, must, could)
    → **WILL** (though CAN is also considered acceptable by some) — the instructor's personal preference is WILL here, though he notes both WILL and CAN could be marked correct depending on the source/expert opinion. MUST is ruled out (not an obligation), and COULD would only fit if the sentence were an especially polite request (which it isn't, since there's no "please").

11. **I ___ hardly take his help.** (Options: could, ought, might, should)
    → **COULD** — "ought" cannot stand alone (needs "to"), so it's eliminated; the correct fit for expressing "I could hardly..." (meaning I was barely able to take his help) is COULD.

12. **When I was in college, I ___ play cricket.** (Options: used to, etc.)
    → **USED TO** — direct past habit, clearly indicated by "when I was in college."

## 22. Key Takeaways for the Exam

1. **CAN is the only modal verb for ability/capability** — no other modal can replace it for this specific meaning.
2. **MUST is the most frequently tested modal** in CUET and similar exams — master its two uses: (a) strong obligation/no other option, and (b) logical certainty/speculation.
3. **Hints in the sentence matter a lot** — words like "immediately," "in my childhood," "when I was young," etc., guide you toward the correct modal. A good question paper will usually include such hints to help you determine the correct sense (ability, possibility, advice, permission, etc.).
4. **Guidelines/laws/manuals always take MUST**, not SHOULD, in formal/mandatory contexts.
5. **SHOULD is the "weakest" of the obligation-type modals** — used for casual/general advice; MUST and OUGHT TO carry more weight/urgency.
6. **WOULD replaces WILL when the situation is hypothetical, unreal, or uncertain** — if you are sure/certain, use WILL; if it's imagined/speculative, use WOULD.
7. **WILL/SHALL rule reversal**: Normally, SHALL goes with I/We and WILL with everyone else — but this **reverses** under determination, threat, or promise (WILL then goes with I/We).
8. **WOULD vs. USED TO for past habits:** WOULD needs a preceding time-context hint; USED TO can stand alone and directly signal past habit.
9. Some exam questions may have **two seemingly correct options** (e.g., can/could, will/can) — in such ambiguous cases, different experts may have different opinions, but such questions are relatively rare. Focus on mastering the core, unambiguous rules first.
10. Do NOT mix up modals just because a sentence "sounds okay" with multiple options — always analyze the **sense/intent** of the sentence (ability? permission? advice? obligation? possibility? habit? hypothetical?) before choosing.
